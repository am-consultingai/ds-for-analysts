import pandas as pd

CUTOFF = pd.Timestamp("2026-01-01")          # the day the prediction is made

events = pd.read_parquet("product_events.parquet")
events = events[events.ts < CUTOFF]          # nothing after the cutoff. ever.

features = (
    events
    .groupby("account_id")
    .agg(
        logins_last_30d=("ts", lambda s: (s > CUTOFF - pd.Timedelta("30D")).sum()),
        media_sources_count=("media_source", "nunique"),
        support_tickets_open=("ticket_open", "sum"),
    )
    .reset_index()
)

Questions we get

Do they need to know Python?
No, and we do not teach it. Your analysts learn to read code, judge it and ask for changes. The AI writes it. What they take home is real Python — pandas and scikit-learn — that runs on its own.
Why scikit-learn and not something heavier?
Because the bottleneck at AppsFlyer is not model capacity, it is framing. scikit-learn fits on a laptop, trains in seconds, and its API is stable enough that an agent writes it correctly first time. A gradient-boosted tree on a well-framed problem beats a deep model on a badly framed one, every time. If a project later needs more, the eight steps do not change.
Will anything go to production?
Day two is for learning and experimenting, so people finish with something solid and a lot of confidence. Real projects start after, when someone chooses one and takes it further. That is the point of the follow-up clinic.
How do we keep the momentum after the day?
Three light things work well: a clinic three to four weeks out where people bring what they built, a few office-hour slots for the groups pushing hardest, and a short showcase at a guild meeting. Two visible results tend to bring the rest along.
What data do they work on?
Our recommendation is a synthetic twin: built to your schema, your account sizes and your seasonality, with no real customer in it. It is quick to approve, and we can plant realistic traps in it on purpose, which teaches better than clean data. An anonymised extract of your own data also works if it clears privacy in time.
Our seventeen are at different levels. Will everyone keep up?
Yes. The eight steps have natural stopping points, and people work in groups of three or four grouped by product, so business context is shared. Finishing at a clean plan and an honest baseline is a real result — it is already more than a hand-written rule gives you.
What if the model is no better than the rule we use today?
That is a useful answer, and the workshop teaches people to say it clearly. Knowing your current rule is close to the best available is worth the two days on its own, and it tells you where to spend effort instead.
Can we cover other questions too?
Groups that get their baseline done early can take a stretch track: what separates trials that convert, or which customers of one product are a good fit for another. Both use the same eight steps.
Can product data scientists join?
They are very welcome, and they make excellent group anchors. Worth deciding together on the call how many.
What do people need on their laptops?
Python, Claude Code and permission to install packages, ready a week before day two. We send a short setup note and check it in advance.
Hebrew or English?
Materials are in English, which keeps the technical terms consistent. Delivery can be in either.
